\begin{abstract}
This document describes a small, dependency-free Common Lisp Transformer that
learns to place new medical-evidence observations in an existing two-dimensional
UMAP atlas.  AWRS--SMC first searches for a useful feature representation and a
winning UMAP.  The trainer then learns the numerical mapping from the selected
features to the coordinates preserved from that winning Common Lisp run.  The
current implementation trains the entire encoder by backpropagation, serializes
the model and preprocessing information as an S-expression, and predicts a new
location without fitting UMAP again.  Results reported here are a technical pilot,
not evidence that the learned atlas has been clinically validated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Purpose and scope}

Ordinary UMAP is non-parametric in this project: adding observations requires the
embedding to be recomputed if coordinates for the new observations are needed.  A
parametric mapping instead learns a function
\[
  f_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{6}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2},
\]
where the input contains the six features selected by AWRS--SMC and the output is
a location in the fixed winning atlas.  Once $f_{\theta}$ has been trained, a new
observation can be projected with one forward pass.

This separation is important.  AWRS--SMC remains responsible for searching over
feature inclusion, transformations, UMAP geometry, clustering quality, and the
adjacency preference.  The Transformer does not repeat that search.  It distills
the winning mapping so that it can be reused.

\section{From deliberate practice to rapid placement}

A useful explanation for a non-specialist is to compare the two stages with
deliberate practice.  During deliberate practice, a person repeatedly performs a
difficult task, receives feedback, and gradually retains a skill that can later be
used with less effort.  In this project, AWRS--SMC performs the expensive stage.
It proposes many alternatives, fits and evaluates candidate UMAPs, and uses the
V-measure, the adjacency potential, and the other terms in its score to select a
winning atlas.  The resulting annotated examples record both the selected input
features and the coordinates produced by that atlas.  They therefore provide the
feedback from which the Transformer can learn.

Training the Transformer moves part of this computational effort from the time of
use to an earlier training phase.  Once training is complete, a new study profile
is standardized with the stored statistics and passed through the network once.
The network predicts its coordinates without rerunning the AWRS--SMC search and
without rebuilding the UMAP.  In machine-learning terminology, this is a form of
\emph{amortized computation}: a substantial cost is paid once so that subsequent
predictions are inexpensive.  It is also an \emph{out-of-sample parametric
mapping}, because one learned function places observations that were not present
when the original atlas was made.

The comparison with human learning is an analogy, not a claim that the program
learns or understands as a person does.  The Transformer compresses a numerical
relationship found in its training corpus.  It receives no new clinical judgment
at prediction time, and rapid placement is trustworthy only to the extent that
the training corpus represents the new observation.  An input outside the stored
feature ranges is an extrapolation and should be flagged for review.

\section{Relation to earlier biomedical and clinical work}

The broad idea has precedents, although the publications use different models and
training objectives.  Parametric UMAP replaces the direct optimization of point
coordinates with a neural network and explicitly supports fast embedding of new
data after training \citep{sainburg2021parametric}.  The closely related
\emph{ivis} method learned a parametric map for single-cell expression and mass
cytometry data and demonstrated that new observations could be added to an
existing embedding \citep{szubert2019ivis}.  These are biomedical examples, but
their observations are cells rather than reports in a clinical meta-analysis.

Transformers have also been trained on heterogeneous clinical and
clinicogenomic records before specialization for treatment-response and survival
tasks \citep{arangoargoty2025clinical}.  This supports the general strategy of
performing an expensive learning phase once and then reusing the learned
representation, but that study did not train a Transformer to reproduce the
coordinates of an AWRS--SMC-selected UMAP atlas.  A recent preprint studies the
specific problem of inserting out-of-sample observations into a fixed UMAP and
reports an advantage for parameterized UMAP on increasingly complex data,
including medical images \citep{islam2026outofsample}.  Because it is a 2026
preprint, this evidence should be treated as preliminary.

Thus, fast placement after prior training is an established research direction.
The particular teacher--student arrangement used here---AWRS--SMC constructs and
scores the atlas, its winning coordinates annotate the corpus, and a small
dependency-free Common Lisp Transformer learns that mapping---is a project-specific
implementation.  It should not be presented as clinical validation or as proof
that no earlier system combined comparable components.

\section{Annotated training corpus}

The corpus builder reads the winning AWRS--SMC result and its preserved Common
Lisp coordinates.  It reconstructs the winning selected features, freezes the
column means and scales, and writes one S-expression record per atlas point.  Each
record contains an identifier, study group, split, standardized input vector,
two-dimensional target, cluster assignment, and medical label.

The pilot contains 90 observations from six study groups.  Seventy observations
are marked for training and 20 for validation.  All observations belonging to one
study remain in the same split.  The validation study in the present artifact is
PALETTE.  This prevents windows from one study from appearing on both sides of the
evaluation.

The six selected inputs are survival progress, window start, window length,
cohort-size context, median progression-free-survival context, and objective
response rate, with the exact schema and transformations stored in the corpus.
The corpus is numeric rather than textual; ``token'' below means one named
numerical feature.

\section{Feature-token Transformer}

For feature $i$ with standardized scalar value $x_i$, the initial token state is
\[
  h_i^{(0)}=e_i+w_x x_i+b_x,
\]
where $e_i$ is a learned feature-identity embedding.  Sharing $w_x$ and $b_x$
across positions supplies a common scalar projection, while $e_i$ tells the model
which clinical feature the number represents.

The six token states pass through one scaled dot-product self-attention block.
For query, key, and value projections,
\[
  q_i=W_qh_i,\qquad k_i=W_kh_i,\qquad v_i=W_vh_i,
\]
the attention weights and mixed value are
\[
  a_{ij}=\operatorname{softmax}_j
  \left(\frac{q_i^{\mathsf T}k_j}{\sqrt{d}}\right),\qquad
  z_i=\sum_j a_{ij}v_j.
\]
An output projection, residual connection, and root-mean-square normalization
follow attention.  A two-layer feed-forward block with a hyperbolic-tangent
activation is followed by a second residual connection and normalization.  Mean
pooling over the six encoded tokens gives a fixed-length representation; a linear
regression head returns two coordinates.

The pilot deliberately remains small: one attention block, one attention head,
model dimension 8, and feed-forward dimension 12.  This controls capacity in a
corpus with only 70 training observations.

\section{Optimization and reproducibility}

The current baseline minimizes coordinate mean squared error,
\[
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coordinate}}
  =\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}
    \left\|f_{\theta}(x_n)-y_n\right\|_2^2,
\]
where $y_n$ is the coordinate pair preserved from the winning Common Lisp UMAP.
Scalar automatic differentiation constructs the computation graph during each
forward pass.  Reverse traversal calculates gradients through scalar projection,
self-attention, normalization, the feed-forward block, pooling, and the output
head.  Adam updates every learned parameter.  Thus, this is full
backpropagation---not the frozen-encoder, head-only optimization used by the
earlier miniature text Transformer.

Initialization and record ordering are deterministic.  The baseline uses seed
20260902, 100 epochs, and learning rate 0.002.  The serialized S-expression
contains the complete model, feature schema, preprocessing statistics, split
policy, and training report.  It contains no machine-specific path and requires
only SBCL and repository files.

Before full-corpus training, the implementation must overfit one observation.
The test verifies that the loss falls by at least four orders of magnitude and
below $10^{-6}$.  It then saves and reloads the weights and verifies that the
prediction is unchanged.  This is a focused gradient and serialization test; it
is not a measure of generalization.

\section{Running the implementation}

From the repository root, the baseline is reproduced with:

\begin{verbatim}
sbcl --script smc-trainer/train.lisp \
  smc-trainer/corpus/pilot-parametric-umap.sexp \
  smc-trainer/weights/pilot-coordinate-baseline.sexp 100 0.002d0
\end{verbatim}

A new observation is projected by supplying its six raw values in the stored
feature-schema order:

\begin{verbatim}
sbcl --script smc-trainer/predict.lisp \
  smc-trainer/weights/pilot-coordinate-baseline.sexp \
  '(0.8 0.1 0.2 2.0 1.0 0.3)'
\end{verbatim}

The prediction program reads the means and scales from the trained artifact,
standardizes the raw values, and returns an S-expression containing the two
coordinates and their coordinate-system identifier.

The corpus and model test suites are run with:

\begin{verbatim}
sbcl --script vendor/test-cases/run-tests.lisp \
  smc-trainer/tests.lisp
sbcl --script vendor/test-cases/run-tests.lisp \
  smc-trainer/trainer-tests.lisp
\end{verbatim}

The current corpus suite passes 1,504 assertions.  The model suite verifies an
independent product derivative, one-observation overfitting, serialization
round-trip, artifact metadata, raw-feature standardization, and finite coordinate
prediction.

\section{Limitations}

The Transformer approximates one fixed atlas and inherits its assumptions and
errors.  It does not demonstrate that the UMAP geometry is medically correct, and
it does not estimate treatment effects.  The 90 atlas observations are dependent
windows derived from only six study groups.  Although the group split prevents
direct window leakage, one held-out group is too small a basis for broad claims of
generalization.

The preserved target coordinates were constructed using the whole pilot atlas,
including the validation study.  Consequently, validation measures approximation
of a fixed transductive atlas, not independent validation of the atlas's clinical
structure.  New observations far outside the feature ranges represented in the
corpus are extrapolations and should be marked as such.

\section{Next experiment: neighbourhood preservation}

Coordinate loss requires the model to reproduce an arbitrary orientation and
scale even though the principal scientific interest lies in local relationships.
The next experiment will add a neighbourhood term,
\[
  \mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coordinate}}
  +\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{neighbourhood}}.
\]
Pairs that are neighbours in the winning atlas will be encouraged to remain close
in the learned projection; selected non-neighbour pairs will prevent collapse.
Several fixed values of $\lambda$, including zero, will be compared under the same
initialization and split.  Coordinate RMSE, nearest-neighbour retention, and
cluster agreement will be reported together.  The coordinate-only artifact in
this document is the required control for that comparison.
